Sardine Lab — One fish, then a school

Build a sardine, set dozens swimming, and discover how simple local rules shape a school.

DERAILED / MARINE LIFE / EXPERIMENT NO. 016

One sardine is a fish. Fifty are a question.

At Aquamarine Fukushima, a shimmering school of Japanese sardines moved as though it were a single creature. Nobody seemed to be in charge. What happens if we start with one fish, make a lot more, and give them a handful of rules?

01 / Many fish, one recipe

The generator draws one recognizable sardine using Canvas paths: silver back, pale belly, forked tail, and an eye. We make an array of fish, each with a slightly different size, heading, and tail rhythm. The fish size slider makes them small enough to share the tank.

Each fish keeps its own movement state. Its direction of travel can change all the way around the tank, but we draw it from the side and mirror left to right, so its belly stays down.

02 / Before the school: independence

Switch to Independent. Each fish wanders and steers away from the tank walls, but it does not know where the other sardines are. More fish alone doesn’t create a coordinated school.

Try raising the count, then dropping it to 1 to inspect the individual generator again. If you’d like a closer look, increase the fish size.

03 / A school without a conductor

Turn on Schooling. Each sardine now responds only to nearby fish, using three ideas from the classic Boids simulation:

  • Separation: don’t crowd your closest neighbours.
  • Alignment: turn toward the direction nearby neighbours are moving.
  • Cohesion: steer toward the neighbourhood, not a central leader.

Try reducing alignment to zero. Do the fish still travel together? Try removing cohesion. Do they gradually drift apart? Raise separation and notice how tightly they can pack.

The simulation computes all the neighbour suggestions from the same moment, and only then updates the fish. That prevents the order of the array from accidentally giving some fish an informational advantage.

04 / What this is—and isn’t

There is no invisible leader fish. Every swimmer runs the same local instructions. And the rules aren’t hard-coded fish formations: the patterns emerge from repeated interactions.

This is a playful, intentionally simplified computational model. Real sardines have sensory systems, predators, currents, and three-dimensional movement that our two-dimensional tank doesn’t attempt to reproduce.

If nobody is in charge, who decides where the school goes?
