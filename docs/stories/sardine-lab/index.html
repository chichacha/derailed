Who is leading the sardines? — Sardine Lab

At Aquamarine Fukushima, a school of sardines parted around a ray. My aunt was sure there was a leader.

DERAILED / MARINE LIFE / AN AQUARIUM DETOUR

Who is leading the sardines?

My aunt had a theory. The fish were not taking questions.

01 / One ray. A lot of sardines.

I went to Aquamarine Fukushima with my relatives. At the big Kuroshio tank, I got completely distracted by the sardines. I could have watched them all day. So many little silver fish moving together, changing direction, somehow not crashing into each other.

Then a カラスエイ (pelagic stingray) glided through. The マイワシ (true sardines) parted around it, so smoothly it barely seemed to interrupt the flow. Just like that, a gap appeared—and the school carried on.

I said how much I loved watching them swim together. My aunt had an explanation:

“There must be a good leader.”

— My aunt, at the aquarium

I love this answer. It makes perfect sense from the outside. If a whole crowd moves in sync, surely someone must be calling the shots?

But… which fish? And how would everyone else get the memo in time?

02 / Meet the actual fish

Before making cartoon sardines swim around a browser, I wanted to know a little more about the two animals that started this whole detour.

THE SCHOOL

マイワシ True sardine

Sardinops melanostictus

They gather near the sea surface, from coastal waters to offshore. Look closely: the row of dark spots along their sides has earned them the Japanese nickname 七つ星—“Seven Stars.” They also migrate north and south with the seasons.

Why swim in a school? Possible advantages include safety in numbers, spotting danger sooner, and reproduction. There isn't just one explanation.

THE SURPRISE VISITOR

カラスエイ Pelagic stingray

Pteroplatytrygon violacea

A dark-backed ray that swims through open ocean water rather than spending most of its life along the seabed. It moves with those broad, wing-like fins.

Here's a fun twist: when feeding, it can actually turn belly-up and use its fins to help bring food toward its mouth. Our earlier upside-down sardine suddenly has some competition.

Aquamarine's カラスエイ profile ↗

Aquamarine’s Kuroshio tank deliberately brings small fish and larger fish together to show something of the ocean’s eat-or-be-eaten world. But I didn’t see the ray attack the sardines. What caught my eye was how beautifully the school made space as it passed.

03 / Okay, but who is in charge?

I couldn’t bring the aquarium home. So I drew one sardine, taught it to swim, and made 47 more. Then I gave each fish a way to react to its neighbours.

No conductor. No group chat. No extremely organized fish manager.

04 / Try taking the leader away

Here’s the experiment I’d love to show my aunt:

  1. Watch the school for a bit. Nobody has been assigned as the leader.
  2. Switch from Schooling to Independent. Same fish, same tank. They’re still swimming, but they aren’t paying attention to one another. What happens?
  3. Switch Schooling back on. Do you notice the difference?
  4. Turn down alignment, cohesion, or separation, one at a time. Which rule seems to matter most?

Change the number of sardines, make them smaller, or follow the dotted path of one individual. That little highlighted fish is just the one we’re watching—not the captain.

You can also click the tank to set a shared destination. That’s fun, but remember: giving everyone a common target is different from asking how a group coordinates without one. For that question, keep Free swim selected.

05 / Three rules. No boss.

The simulation is inspired by Boids, a model introduced by computer graphics researcher Craig Reynolds in 1987. It starts with three surprisingly ordinary ideas:

  • Separation: “Excuse me, you’re a bit close.”
  • Alignment: “Oh, we’re going that way?”
  • Cohesion: “Hey, where did everyone go?”

Each fish responds to the neighbours it can ‘see’ in our simplified model. None of them knows the shape of the whole school. Yet a recognizable group can take shape anyway.

That kind of larger pattern arising from lots of small interactions has a name: emergence.

The code is much simpler than a real sardine. Real fish have eyes, senses, three-dimensional water to navigate, currents, and much more going on. This experiment isn’t proof that real マイワシ behave according to exactly these rules. It’s a way to test whether a leader is necessary for coordinated-looking movement.

06 / And what about the カラスエイ?

We haven’t put a ray in our simulated tank. Not yet.

That’s actually the part I’m most curious about. What if a big ray-shaped obstacle moved through the school? Would each sardine reacting locally be enough to make the whole school open up around it?

There are other questions too. Were the fish avoiding the ray itself, the movement of nearby sardines, or a combination of cues? Watching them through the glass didn’t tell me—and this first model can’t tell me either.

But now I have an experiment to try. All because my aunt made one little comment at the aquarium.

“There must be a good leader.”
Maybe. Or maybe the fish just need good neighbours.
