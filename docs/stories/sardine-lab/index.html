At Aquamarine Fukushima, a school of sardines parted around a ray. My aunt was sure there was a leader.

DERAILED / MARINE LIFE / AN AQUARIUM DETOUR

Who is leading the sardines?

My aunt suspected there was a leader. The sardines declined to comment.

01 / One ray. A lot of sardines.

I visited Aquamarine Fukushima with my relatives and found myself completely mesmerized by the sardines in the big Kuroshio tank.

Hundreds of little silver fish, swimming together, turning together, somehow never bumping into one another. I could have watched them for hours.

Then a カラスエイ (pelagic stingray) glided through. The マイワシ (Japanese sardines) scattered, only to regroup and carry on as if nothing had happened.

My aunt had a theory:

“There must be a good leader.”

— My aunt, at the aquarium

I love this answer. It makes perfect sense from the outside. If a whole crowd moves in sync, surely someone must be calling the shots?

But… which fish? And how would everyone else get the memo in time?

02 / Meet the actual fish

Before making cartoon sardines swim around a browser, I wanted to know a little more about the two animals that started this whole detour.

THE SCHOOL

マイワシ True sardine

Sardinops melanostictus

They gather near the sea surface, from coastal waters to offshore. Look closely: the row of dark spots along their sides has earned them the Japanese nickname 七つ星—“Seven Stars.” They also migrate north and south with the seasons.

Why swim in a school? Possible advantages include safety in numbers, spotting danger sooner, and reproduction. There isn't just one explanation.

THE SURPRISE VISITOR

カラスエイ Pelagic stingray

Pteroplatytrygon violacea

A dark-backed ray that swims through open ocean water rather than spending most of its life along the seabed. It moves with those broad, wing-like fins.

Here's a fun twist: when feeding, it can actually turn belly-up and use its fins to help bring food toward its mouth. Our earlier upside-down sardine suddenly has some competition.

Aquamarine's カラスエイ profile ↗

Aquamarine Fukushima’s Kuroshio tank recreates a little of the ocean’s eat-or-be-eaten world, bringing predators and prey together. Pelagic stingrays do eat sardines, but the one I watched wasn’t visibly hunting. It simply glided through, and the school parted around it, then flowed back together.

What fascinated me wasn’t the predator. It was how effortlessly hundreds of little fish responded together.

03 / Could I make a school without a leader?

My aunt’s theory stayed with me. When hundreds of fish change direction together, it really does look as though someone must be giving instructions.

But what if nobody is?

I couldn’t bring the aquarium home, so I made a small version in my browser. First I drew one sardine. I gave it a direction, a speed, and a tail that moved. Then I made 47 more.

Forty-eight swimming fish are not automatically a school. Without paying attention to one another, they’re simply 48 individuals sharing a tank.

That gave me a better question: what does each fish need to notice about its neighbours for a school to appear?

05 / Three little problems with swimming together

I found it easier to understand the model by thinking about three ordinary problems. None requires a clever fish in charge. Each only requires a fish to pay attention to its neighbours.

Problem one: “You’re too close!” — Separation

Imagine walking through a crowded station. Someone steps into your personal space, and you move aside.

Separation is the fish version of that. In my model, a sardine turns away from neighbours that get too close. It doesn’t need to know where the whole school is headed; it only needs to avoid crowding the fish beside it.

Try it: With Schooling selected, slide Separation down to zero, then bring it back up. Look for changes in how closely the fish bunch together.

Problem two: “Oh, we’re going that way?” — Alignment

Now imagine walking with a group of friends. They’re all beginning to turn left, so you turn a little left too.

Alignment helps neighbouring fish face similar directions. Each fish considers the headings of fish nearby and adjusts its own. Nobody sends out a message saying, “Everyone turn now.”

Try it: Turn Alignment down to zero, then increase it again. Watch whether nearby fish tend to point the same way.

Problem three: “Hey, wait for me!” — Cohesion

If you get distracted while walking with friends, eventually you notice they’re farther away. You head back toward them.

Cohesion does something similar. Each simulated fish steers toward the average position of its nearby neighbours. It isn’t chasing a designated leader—just trying not to lose the group.

Try it: Reduce Cohesion to zero, then restore it. Does the group seem more scattered or more connected?

These rules work together, so changing one slider won’t always produce an instant, tidy result. The tank is a place to observe, not a machine that guarantees the same pattern every time.

06 / The surprising part: nobody needs the whole picture

This experiment is inspired by Boids, a model introduced by computer graphics researcher Craig Reynolds in 1987. The three rules are simple: give your neighbours room, pay attention to their direction, and stay near the group.

Here’s the lovely part. No individual fish has to know what the whole school looks like. Each reacts to nearby fish, yet a larger, coordinated-looking pattern can appear.

That has a name: emergence—a group-level pattern arising from many local interactions.

Does that prove my aunt was wrong about the real sardines? No. A two-dimensional browser model isn’t a real aquarium. Real fish use their senses, navigate three-dimensional water, and respond to far more than three rules.

But it shows something important: a leader isn’t necessary to produce movement that looks coordinated.

07 / And then there was the stingray

Now I’m back at the moment that started this detour: the カラスエイ gliding through the マイワシ, and the school opening around it.

My simulated tank doesn’t have a stingray. Not yet.

Would a moving obstacle be enough to make a similar gap appear? Would the fish react directly to the ray, to their neighbours’ reactions, or both? I can’t answer that from what I saw through the glass—and this first experiment can’t either.

Which gives me another question to investigate. One aquarium visit, one comment from my aunt, and somehow I’ve ended up teaching 48 imaginary sardines to be good neighbours.

“There must be a good leader.”
Maybe. Or maybe the fish just need good neighbours.
